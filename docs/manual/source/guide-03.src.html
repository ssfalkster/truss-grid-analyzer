In step 2 you put the loads on the trusses: fixtures from the library, weights you type, and spread loads. Every load is a point load at one position; for eight lights, add eight loads (or four, mirrored).

Mirrored loads

Rigs are often symmetrical. Tick Mirror on a load and the truss carries the same load at the same distance on the other side of its centerline. The twin is not a separate load: change the original's weight or position and the twin follows. A load exactly on the centerline has no twin. On the plan the twin's triangle is drawn lighter.

Copying loads to another truss

Copy to truss… (in the grid group header or the truss panel) asks for the name of the truss to copy to, then copies every load keeping its distance from the centre - so the pattern stays centred on a truss of a different length. Loads that would fall past the end of the other truss are placed at its end, and the app tells you how many.

Working with the plan strip

While a cell in the Loads grid is active, the plan shrinks to a strip showing just that truss: its centerline (CL), corner blocks, hoists and loads, with the load you are editing highlighted. Click Show plan (Esc) in the strip to bring the full plan back, or untick Collapse plan while typing in the grid header to keep the plan full size.

Get the weights right

A load with no weight is listed in Check rig. Remember cable, clamps, safeties, motors on moving trusses and anything else that will hang there: use a category load factor or the cable allowance for what you cannot list.

Step 3 - Hoists

In step 3 you place the hoists (and dead hangs), choose their models and chains, and see their loads live. A new truss has no hoists.

[[img step3.jpg|Step 3: each hoist on the plan shows its high hook static load and, in italics, its high hook dynamic load. The grid lists every hoist with its results.|100]]

Adding hoists

  • In the grid: click the At cell of the + hoist on … row, type the position and press Enter. The cursor stays on the new row, so you can type the next position straight away.
  • With the button: select the truss and click + Hoist (above the plan, or at the top of the truss panel). The hoist is added in the middle of the truss; type its position in its panel or drag it.
  • On a corner block: select the block, open Supports and click + Add hoist.
  • By mirroring or copying other hoists - see below.

A new hoist is set up like the last hoist you placed in the rig - same model, chain (or rope), hardware and dynamic factor - so a rig of identical hoists takes no extra typing. The very first hoist in a rig is a 1-ton CM Lodestar with 20 ft of chain.

Moving a hoist

Type its position (At and From in the grid, or Position in its panel), or drag it along its truss on the plan. While you drag, the position is shown next to the hoist and the rig is re-solved as it moves. It moves in 1" steps (2 cm in metric), counted from the truss's measuring reference, so the distance stays a round number. Clicking a hoist without dragging just selects it.

Mirroring and copying hoists

  • Mirror (in a hoist's panel) adds a hoist at the mirror position on the other side of the truss's centerline.
  • Mirror all (truss panel, or the grid group header) does this for every hoist on the truss.
  • Copy to… (hoist panel) and Copy to truss… (truss panel, grid) copy hoists to another truss - you type its name - keeping each one's distance from the centre. Hoists past the end are placed at the end.

Unlike mirrored loads, each copy is a real, separate hoist you can edit on its own. It gets the same model, chain or rope, dynamic factor, hardware, level offset and Hangs from, but not the name or a load-cell reading. No copy is placed on the centerline or where a hoist already is; the app tells you what it left out.

Hoist models and the dynamic factor

The hoist list has 76 built-in chain hoists from the original workbook (CM, Chainmaster, Stagemaker, Prolyte and others) and any you add under Databases. Each has a capacity, a speed, a body weight and a chain weight per foot.

The dynamic factor allows for the extra load while a hoist starts, stops and runs. For a hoist with a listed speed it is speed in ft/min ÷ 60 + 1 (16 fpm = 1.267, 32 fpm = 1.533). A hoist with no listed speed (for example a custom hoist) uses the rig's Default dynamic factor (1.25 unless you change it in Rig settings). You can type any factor for one hoist in its DLF / Dyn. factor box. A hoist's status compares its static high hook load with its capacity; the dynamic load is flagged separately if it exceeds capacity.

Dead hangs

A dead hang is a support made of wire rope and hardware from the truss to the hang point, with no hoist. To make one, set the hoist's Support to Dead hang (wire rope, no hoist), or type dead in the grid's Hoist cell. Then set:

FieldWhat to enter
RopeThe rope: 7x19 galvanized aircraft cable (1/8" to 3/8") or 6x19 IWRC EIPS (1/4" to 1/2"), each shown with its WLL. Or not in the list - WLL typed below. A new dead hang starts as 3/8" GAC.
Rope lengthFor its weight (and its stretch, if the rig's hoists are given a stiffness).
Assembly WLLThe working load limit of the weakest part of the assembly (a shackle, a fitting). It caps the rope's WLL.
HardwareShackles, beam clamp, spanset…

The WLL is the rope's minimum breaking strength ÷ the design factor (10:1 by default; 7:1 or 8:1 in Rig settings), capped by the assembly WLL. The panel writes out the sum. Breaking strengths are typical catalogue values - check your rope's certificate. A dead hang with no rope and no WLL is Overloaded. On the plan a dead hang is a filled dot; on the calc sheet it is DH1, DH2….

Hoists hung below a truss

Sometimes a truss is flown from hoists that hang from another truss rather than from the building. Set such a hoist's Hangs from to below the upper truss (the carrier). Then:

  • The hoist hooks onto the carrier where the plan puts it - so it must be directly under the carrier on the plan (the panel tells you where it hooks on, and warns if it is off to the side).
  • Its chain joins the two trusses in the whole-rig analysis, so the carrier sags with the load and the truss below shares load with its other hoists. The chain can only pull.
  • The carrier carries the hung hoist's high hook load, and the carrier (with everything bolted to it) is checked with the hung hoist's high hook dynamic load. So are the hoists holding the carrier up, unless you turn that off in Rig settings.
  • On the plan the hung hoist is a dashed ring. The rig's total high hook load counts only hoists to the structure.

Hoist levels

Two different things:

  • Level offset (per hoist) - a designed difference: a truss hung raked or trimmed on purpose, or a dead hang whose length is fixed. Type inches (mm), + higher. The whole-rig analysis solves the rig with that point moved, and the loads shift accordingly.
  • Out-of-level tolerance (Rig settings) - unintended error: any hoist may end up that far off (say ±1/4"). Every hoist's check then carries the worst it could see, and a hoist that could unload completely is warned about.
Level sensitivity matters on stiff rigs

On short, stiff spans, and in grids with many hoists, a small level error moves a lot of load. The app warns when a 1/4" error would change a hoist's load by more than 10% of its capacity. Level such hoists carefully, use load cells, or set an out-of-level tolerance so the checks include it. Giving hoists a stiffness (Rig settings) makes the analysis more realistic and much less level-sensitive.

Measured loads (load cells)

If you have load cells, type each reading in the Measured column (or the hoist panel's Load cell reading). The app compares it with the calculated high hook static load - or with the low hook load if your cells hang between the hoist and the truss (set Load cells read in Rig settings). The vs calc. column shows the difference in percent. The rig panel's Measured vs calculated section totals each assembly of joined trusses and flags a total more than 5% off. One hoist on a rig that shares load can differ much more than that (levels, stiffness) - compare the assembly totals. Readings never change a result.

A truss's Checks section

Select a truss and open Checks in its panel to see exactly why it passes or fails. From the top:

  1. Elevation - the truss from the side. Each span and cantilever is labelled with its length and coloured (green OK, red failing, with the failure named).   2. Where it works hardest - a strip shaded by local workload (green through amber to red), with the peak marked and named.
  3. Reactions - arrows to scale at each support. At hoists this is the low hook load; at bolts the force passed to the carrier. A red downward arrow means the truss is pushing up there.
  4. Shear and Moment diagrams - with the estimated allowable as dashed lines and the maximum against it.
  5. Deflection - the deflected shape, exaggerated, with each span's sag in inches and as span/ratio, and the limit used (the maker's, with a link to the data sheet, or the rig default).
  6. Bars for Span load, Cantilever, Moment, Shear and Deflection.
  7. Chips: the total load on the truss, the repetitive-use derate used, and Checked with … joint model.

Check rig

Click an empty part of the plan to show the rig panel. Its Check rig section lists input mistakes the calculation would otherwise take at face value:

  • a load with no weight;
  • two hoists at the same point (closer than 2") - is one a duplicate?;
  • a bolted assembly with fewer than 3 hoists, or with all its hoists in one line (it could tip about that line);
  • bolted ends that do not meet on the plan;
  • trusses that cross on the plan with no joint between them (a note: they may be at different heights);
  • a hoist with no model.

What to do when something fails

ProblemThings to try
A hoist is OverloadedAdd a hoist nearby, move hoists to share the load, move or lighten loads, use a bigger hoist. Check the hoist's Where the load comes from table.
A span is OverloadedAdd a hoist in the span (shorter span, more capacity), move point loads toward supports, use a stronger truss.
A span or cantilever is Too longAdd a support or shorten the overhang (cantilever ≤ max span ÷ 4).
Moment or shear overUsually a heavy load right next to a support, or bending over a support from a loaded neighbouring span: move the load or add a hoist.
Deflection overShorten the span or lighten it.
A hoist is SlackIt would be pushed up - usually next to a heavy cantilever or span. Move it, or move the load; the other hoists carry more meanwhile.
UnstableThe truss or assembly has too few supports or they are in a line. Add hoists.
Not solvedIt has no supports, or is bolted to something missing, or is part of a loop. See the warning.
