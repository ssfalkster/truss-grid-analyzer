Introducing Truss Grid Analyzer

Truss Grid Analyzer works out the load on every chain hoist in a truss rig and checks every truss against its manufacturer's published limits. You describe the rig in four steps:

  1. Structure - the trusses: their make and model, length, where they sit on the plan, and how they are bolted together at corner blocks.
  2. Loads - what hangs on them: lights from a library of fixtures, or any weight you type, plus spread loads such as a drape or an LED wall.
  3. Hoists - where the rig hangs from: chain hoists (or wire-rope dead hangs), their models and chain lengths.
  4. Results - the load on every hoist, every truss check, and every warning.

Everything is recalculated the moment you change anything, so you can try ideas - move a hoist, add a light - and see the effect straight away.

What It Checks

  • Hoists: the high hook load (the truss load on the hook plus the hoist, its chain and hardware) against the hoist's rated capacity, and the dynamic load (static × a factor for the hoist's speed).
  • Truss spans: the point loads on every span against the manufacturer's load table for that span length, with the ANSI repetitive-use factor (0.85) unless the table already includes it.
  • Cantilevers: the overhang past the last support: no longer than a quarter of the maximum span, and no more load than the table allows.
  • Bending moment and shear: the whole length of each truss, which catches bending over supports and heavy loads next to a support that a span-by-span check misses.
  • Deflection: how far each span sags, against the maker's published limit where there is one (otherwise a default of span/160).
  • The rig as a whole: a whole-rig analysis works out how trusses bolted into a grid share load, including hoists that go slack and assemblies that would tip.
  • Input mistakes: a load with no weight, two hoists at one point, bolted ends that do not meet, parts from different truss families bolted together, and more.

What You Need to Know First

A calculation aid, not an engineering approval

This tool is intended for entertainment rigging professionals to help them make rigging decisions. Every attempt has been made to be accurate, but the authors are not responsible for errors in the program or the results of its use. You are responsible for verifying the results before using them to make rigging decisions. Check results against the manufacturer's current data, and have a qualified person review every rig before it flies.

  • The results are only as good as what you enter. Weigh or look up every load, and include cable, clamps and safeties (the app has allowances for these - see Rig Settings).
  • Some figures are estimates and the app says so: truss stiffness, the allowable bending moment and shear, and deflection are worked out from the manufacturers' load tables, not published values.
  • The app does not check the structure the hoists hang from (beams, roof steel, a ground-support system) - only the hoists and the trusses.
Indeterminate rigs (ANSI E1.6-3)

ANSI E1.6-3 (Selection and Use of Serially Manufactured Chain Hoists in the Entertainment Industry, section 2.11) calls a load system indeterminate when the way its load divides between the hoists depends on the stiffness of the structure as well as on where the loads and hoists are. In practice that is any rig on more than two hoists in a straight line, or more than three hoists not in a line: a truss on three hoists, and almost every grid or box. On these rigs load can shift between hoists as they run or if one is out of level, and the standard warns that stiff indeterminate structures can shift load very quickly.

Section 7.1 of the standard accepts a load calculation as a way to reduce the risk of overload only when all the weights are known and accounted for, an accurate calculation method is used, and any load shifting will not be enough to cause an overload. The whole-rig analysis in this app is built for these rigs - it solves the grid with hinged, semi-rigid and rigid joints and uses the worst - but it is still an estimate. Allow a margin for load shifting, level hoists carefully, and verify the loads (for example with load cells) where the margin is small.

Where It Came From

Truss Grid Analyzer, by G.E. Simmons Falk, is an expansion of Truss Load Analyzer - EOT, the Excel workbook created by Delbert L. Hall and Jon Sogoian, who are the originators of the program, for indeterminate grids. The workbook works truss by truss; Truss Grid Analyzer extends it to trusses bolted into grids and boxes and to trusses on three or more hoists - rigs whose hoist loads depend on the stiffness of the trusses and joints, not on the load positions alone (see Indeterminate rigs above). It uses their method and their truss, hoist and fixture data, adds manufacturers' own load tables, and adds the grid model, the whole-rig analysis, and the plan and 3D views. It is free, and must never be sold (see Credits, License and Disclaimer).

Getting Started

What You Need

  • A computer with a current web browser: Google Chrome, Microsoft Edge, Mozilla Firefox or Apple Safari. A large screen (a laptop or bigger) works best; the app is not designed for phones.
  • The app file. The whole app is a single file called index.html - on the project's GitHub page it is dist/index.html. It contains everything: the program, the truss, hoist, corner block and fixture databases.

There is nothing to install, no account, and no internet connection is needed. The app never sends your rig anywhere. (The only things that go online are the links to manufacturers' data sheets, which open in a new browser tab when you click them.)

Opening the App

  1. Save index.html somewhere you will find it again, for example a Truss Grid Analyzer folder in Documents. You can rename it (for example Truss Grid Analyzer {{VER}}.html).
  2. Double-click it. It opens in your default browser. (Or drag it onto an open browser window.)
  3. Check the version at the top left, next to Truss Grid Analyzer.

The first time you open it, the app shows the example box - a small box truss with two inner trusses, loads and twelve hoists - so you have something to explore. You can bring it back any time with File > Load example box.

Where Your Work Is Kept

The app saves every change automatically inside the web browser on your computer. Close the browser, come back tomorrow, and your rig (and your custom trusses, hoists and fixtures) will be there.

Automatic saving is not a backup
  • It is kept by this browser on this computer. Another browser, another computer or a private/incognito window starts empty (with the example box).
  • Clearing the browser's history or "site data" can erase it.
  • It holds one rig at a time - the one on screen. Starting a new rig or opening another replaces it (Undo brings it back only until you close or reload the page).

So keep every rig you care about as a file with File > Save (see Files, Export and Sharing).

Starting a New Rig

Choose File > New rig and click OK. The plan is cleared and you start at step 1.

Save first

File > New rig, File > Open… and File > Load example box each replace the rig on screen. Each is one Undo step, so Ctrl+Z brings the old rig back - but only until you close or reload the page. Use File > Save before any of them to keep a copy.

Naming the Rig

Click the rig's name in the top bar (a new rig is called Untitled rig), type a new name and click OK. You can also edit it at the top of the rig panel (click an empty part of the plan to show it). The name is used for the saved file, the calculation sheet and exported pictures.

Dark or Light

The app starts in a dark theme, which is easy on the eyes in a dark venue. Choose File > Theme > Light for daylight or an office, or Follow system to match your computer's setting. The same choice is in Rig Settings. The theme is remembered on this computer; it is not saved in rig files and does not change any result.

Undo and Redo

Almost every change can be undone: click ↶ / ↷ in the top bar, or press Ctrl+Z to undo and Ctrl+Y (or Ctrl+Shift+Z) to redo. The app keeps the last 100 changes. A paste, a multi-hoist edit, New rig, Open and Load example box each count as one change. The history is lost when you close or reload the page. (Undo does not work while you are typing in a box - finish the entry first.)

The Steps Bar

[[img steps.png|The steps bar, the add button and the view toolbar.|70]]

The four steps split the work into manageable parts. Click a step (or press 1, 2, 3 or 4) to change the grid under the plan and the add button. You can go to any step at any time and in any order - the steps do not lock anything. Each step shows a count: the number of trusses, loads and hoists, and on Results a tick (✓, all OK), ▲n (n warnings) or ✕n (n things overloaded or unsolved). The step number turns green when that step has something in it (for Hoists: when there are hoists and nothing is left to check); the step you are on is highlighted.

The add button next to the steps does the obvious thing for the step: + Truss (step 1), + Load or + Hoist (steps 2 and 3, on the selected truss - if no truss is selected it adds a new truss instead), and Calc sheet (step 4).

The Plan

The plan shows the rig from above, drawn to scale on a grid, with north at the top. The thin dark lines marked X 0 and Y 0 are the origin. Each truss is drawn as a colored bar with its name and length; corner blocks are dark squares; hoists are rings with cross-hairs, labeled with their loads; loads are small triangles labeled with their weight. See Plan and 3D Views for everything you can do on it.

Under the plan is a spreadsheet for the current step: every truss (step 1), every load grouped by truss (step 2), or every hoist grouped by truss (step 3), with results updating as you type. Step 4 shows the results tables instead. The grid works like Excel - see Working in the Grids.

Resizing the Panels

Drag the border between the plan and the side panel, or between the plan and the grid, to make either bigger. Double-click a border to put it back. The app remembers your layout on this computer.

Key Ideas

A few words have a specific meaning in the app. The Glossary has the full list.

Trusses and Truss Lines

In the app, a truss is one straight line of truss - which may be several sections (pieces) bolted end to end, and may include corner blocks along it. For example a 32 ft line might be a corner block, 30 ft of truss in 10 ft and 5 ft sticks, and another corner block. The app treats the whole line as one continuous beam.

Each truss has a start and an end. On the plan, the start is at the truss's X, Y position, and the truss runs from there in the direction of its angle: 0° runs east (to the right), 90° north (up), 180° west and 270° south.

Corner Blocks and Bolting

A corner block is a short block that trusses bolt to at right angles (a 6-way block has six faces: four sides, top and bottom). In the app a corner block always belongs to one truss - it is part of that truss line, at its start, its end or somewhere along it. Other trusses are then bolted to the block. A bolted truss is positioned by its block: it moves when the block's truss moves, and you cannot drag it on its own.

A truss can be free (you place it by typing its X, Y and angle, or dragging it) or bolted (placed by a corner block). A truss can also be stacked on top of another truss or clamped below it at the point where they cross.

Measuring Positions: Start, Centerline or End

Every load and hoist position is a distance along its truss, measured from one of three places:

  • start - from the start of the whole line (0 = the start end);
  • centerline (CL) - from the middle, negative toward the start and positive toward the end (-12, +12);
  • end - from the far end of the line.

Each truss has a Measure from setting (a new truss uses the centerline), and each load or hoist can use its own. Pick whichever matches the drawing you are working from. Lengths are measured along the whole line, including corner blocks.

Loads

A load is a point load at one position: a fixture from the library (with its clamp), or any weight you type. A mirrored load also puts the same weight at the mirror-image position on the other side of the centerline, and follows any change you make to the original. A UDL (uniformly distributed load) is a weight spread evenly over the whole length of a truss - a drape, an LED wall hung along it, or cable.

Hoists, Low Hook and High Hook

A hoist is a support point where a chain hoist holds the truss (or, as a dead hang, a wire rope with no hoist). The app uses these words throughout:

TermMeaning
Low hook loadWhat the truss hangs on the hoist's hook (sometimes written R, the reaction).
High hook loadThe low hook load + any Add % + the weight of the hoist, its chain and hardware: what the structure above carries.
Static / dynamicStatic is the load hanging still. Dynamic is static × the dynamic factor for the hoist's movement (for a hoist with a listed speed: speed in ft/min ÷ 60 + 1, so a 16 fpm hoist is 1.267).
WorkloadA load as a percentage of what is allowed: for a hoist, high hook static ÷ rated capacity; for a truss, the highest of its span, cantilever, moment, shear and deflection checks.
Level sensitivityHow much a hoist's load changes if it runs 1/4" (6 mm) high or low compared with the others.

Spans and Cantilevers

A span is the part of a truss between two supports (hoists or bolted connections). A cantilever is the part past the last support at either end. Manufacturers publish how much load each span length can carry; the app reads those tables. A cantilever may be no longer than a quarter of the truss's maximum span.

The Whole-Rig Analysis

When trusses are bolted into a grid, they share load: a truss sags a little, and some of its load moves to neighboring hoists. The app works this out with a whole-rig analysis (a stiffness or finite-element analysis of the grid). Because nobody publishes how stiff the joint through a corner block is, the app solves the rig several ways - with the joints hinged, semi-rigid and rigid - and every check uses the worst result. You do not need to do anything to make this happen; see How the App Calculates if you want to know more.

Weights and Other Numbers

Weights are typed as plain numbers in the rig's units (pounds, or kilograms in metric). In the load grid's Item cell and the quick-add box you can include the unit: 45 lb or 20 kg. Other units the app uses: lb/ft (kg/m) for weight per length, inches (mm) for level offsets and truss widths, lb/in (kg/mm) for hoist stiffness, ft/min (m/min) for hoist speed, lb-ft (kg·m) for moments.

Metric or Imperial

Choose Units in Rig Settings. Switching changes only what is shown and typed: the rig is stored and calculated the same way, so no result and no truss or hoist choice changes. The setting is saved with the rig. Trusses whose manufacturer data is metric are always checked against their own metric table.

Committing a Value

A value takes effect when you press Enter or Tab, or click somewhere else. Results update straight away.
